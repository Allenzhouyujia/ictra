How this project got here, and what happens next

A noisy home-built board, a public clinical dataset, and a written record of every version we tried, including the ones the rules rejected.

Part I · What we did

Every step below has a number behind it and a link to where the evidence lives.

  1. Before 17 Oct

    A board, then a better dataset

    We built a 10-bit Arduino board that reads EEG, ECG, EMG and motion. It is noisy: 58–60 % of its samples sit at the converter's limits. It proves the idea can be wired and streamed, not that it detects seizures. Model results therefore come from SeizeIT2, a public clinical dataset of 125 patients, 11,626 hours and 883 seizures, recorded with behind-the-ear EEG and wired ECG, EMG and accelerometer.

    The hardware →
  2. 17 Oct

    Fixing the data before trusting the model

    The first real files broke four assumptions. Sensor dropouts, where one or two samples are pulled to zero, became 50–200 µV spikes after filtering. Accelerometer scale differed between recordings, so every run is now calibrated to 1 g at rest. A training script could have silently removed ECG from Model B. And the false-alarm count included hours that should have been masked. All four were fixed before a single feature table was kept.

  3. 17 Oct

    Measure the timing before building on it

    In a study of 695 development seizures, focal-to-bilateral tonic-clonic seizures showed the EEG change first, followed by heart rate, accelerometer and EMG (median lags of +3.5 to +12 s). Our first hypothesis, that EEG leads heart rate in most seizures, was not supported (41–56 % by group). We narrowed it into two testable hypotheses and wrote them into the pre-registration draft before any locked-test data were opened. A second study looked for pre-seizure drift in heart rate, HRV, EMG and movement. None of the 20 tests passed, so we claim detection, not prediction.

    AI & method →
  4. 17 Oct

    The first model, and its first honest number

    Version 1 combined all four signals in one gradient-boosted model. It reached 0.44 sensitivity and an event F1 of 0.32 on development folds, and adding ECG, EMG and motion improved F1 by 0.064 over EEG alone (probability of improvement 0.998). Its false-alarm rate, however, was measured only on recordings that contain a seizure, which flattered it. We wrote that caveat into the results instead of hiding it.

    Version 1 results →
  5. 17–18 Oct

    The versions that failed

    Version 3 chose the alarm threshold by inner cross-validation. It halved false alarms, but sensitivity fell by six points, and the rule we wrote in advance rejects it: sensitivity may not drop by more than 0.02 for a gain in F1. The rejection is logged with its numbers. We then corrected the protocol. The realistic baseline is version 2 on all 8,642 development hours, and models are compared at fixed false-alarm budgets (1, 2, 5 and 10 per 24 hours) rather than at each model's own best threshold.

  6. 18 Oct

    Showing the record

    The website went live on 18 October with the full record: the rejected version, the caveats, and the plan for the test. The concept images are labelled as concept renders. They are not photographs of hardware or patients.

Part II · Now

18 October 2026

Version 2 (all 8,642 development hours) is training. It should finish on 19 October. Version 3b, a re-run under the amended protocol, is queued behind it. The locked test (29 patients, sub-097 to sub-125) has not been opened.

Training runs on a laptop, so timings are estimates: it slows whenever the machine sleeps or a game is open.

Part III · Next (planned)

Dates are targets. Each model change is one change, judged by the rule written before it runs.

  1. 19 Oct

    Read version 2 at fixed budgets

    Compare version 2 and version 3b at 1, 2, 5 and 10 false alarms per 24 hours, then decide version 3b under the amended rule.

  2. Late Oct

    Next model changes, one at a time

    A 2-second window (v07), k-of-n voting to suppress isolated alarms (v08), relationship features taken from the timing study (v04), a causal version that only uses past samples, closer to real-time (v11), and an on-device export.

  3. By 10 Nov

    Hardware and live demonstration

    The team demonstrates the sensors and the pipeline live. The Arduino board is shown with its limits; the validation numbers come from SeizeIT2.

  4. ~14 Nov

    Freeze

    No more model changes. The pre-registration is hashed and published, so anyone can check that the rules were fixed before the test.

  5. After freeze

    One look at the locked test

    The 29 locked patients are evaluated once. The result is recorded whatever it shows, including a result below the baseline.

  6. After freeze

    Blind third-party evaluation

    A submission to the SzCORE benchmark (Docker, scored by an independent team) and a request to the SeizeIT2 authors at KU Leuven for a hold-out evaluation. Each is submitted once, so the numbers cannot be re-tuned after they arrive.

  7. By 29 Nov

    Patent decision

    The method is already described publicly on this site, which limits some claims abroad. The decision is time-sensitive, and it depends on the freeze results.

The rule behind every step

Write the criterion down before the number arrives. The rejected versions stay in the record, because a project that only shows its successes cannot be checked.
