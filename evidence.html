Three answers before the detail

What helped. Adding ECG, EMG and motion to EEG cut false alarms from 0.86 to 0.04 a day at the same sensitivity, on 29 patients the model had never seen. That was the project’s primary hypothesis, hashed before the test was opened, and it held on every bootstrap sample.

Under what control. Patient-wise development folds, then one run of a locked internal test that is now spent. The threshold, the features and the analysis script were frozen and hashed before the locked patients were downloaded.

What it still cannot show. Performance on anyone outside SeizeIT2, since the two external evaluations have not been run; any on-device result; or prediction before a seizure. These results are for detection. And one of our own development findings — that the brain signal changes first — did not replicate on the locked patients.

Training data and held-out evaluation

SeizeIT2 is the only training set: the only public dataset with EEG, ECG and EMG recorded simultaneously from one wearable, in the two-channel behind-the-ear setting our hardware targets. Everything else is a test the model never sees during training, opened once after a hashed pre-registration, the way a landslide model trained on one continent is tested on another.

The pre-registration, and exactly what its timestamp proves

Before the locked recordings were downloaded we wrote down what we would test and how we would judge it, and took a SHA-256 of that document:

bc5048c88ae3524348215e4c62801ec66fedbe5c4e4ac616bcbb3c3944fedca6

The document and its hash are public in our research repository, in this commit, alongside a second commit holding a SHA-256 of all 778 annotation files in the locked set, recorded the moment they arrived.

What the public timestamp proves. The repository was pushed on 28 October 2026 at 04:07 UTC. That is before any locked-set feature was extracted and before the test was run, so the rules could not have been written to fit a result we had already seen.

What it does not prove. It does not prove the hash existed before the locked recordings were downloaded, which happened earlier the same day. The commit dates say 26 October, but a local commit date is set by the machine that made it. The strongest supporting evidence we can offer is the repository history: the pre-registration draft, and the hypotheses changing as our own measurements came in, run back to 17 October. We would rather state the limit than let someone find it.

Results status

The locked internal test was opened once on 28 October 2026 and is reported first below, with the hypothesis that failed and the finding that did not replicate. Everything after it is development work, labelled as such. Two external evaluations remain untouched. Every number on this page is re-derivable from the published per-second predictions.

How to read our numbers (plain language)
Sensitivity
Of all seizures that happened, the share the system flagged. 0.44 = 44 seizures in 100 caught.
FA / 24 h
False alarms per day of wearing: alarms with no seizure behind them. A watch that cries wolf ten times a day gets taken off.
Event F1
One number that balances the two: high when most seizures are caught and most alarms are real. 0.32 means neither side is good yet; the SzCORE 2025 winner scored 0.32 on scalp EEG.
Threshold τ
The alarm line. The model outputs a probability every 2 s; an alarm fires when 3 of the last 5 values exceed τ. Raise τ: fewer false alarms, fewer seizures caught. Lower it: the reverse. Every version sits somewhere on this curve.
Fixed FA budget
"At no more than 2 false alarms a day, how many seizures do you catch?" The only fair way to compare two versions or two devices.
FBTC
Focal-to-bilateral tonic-clonic: the convulsive seizure, the type linked to SUDEP. ICTRA catches 0.97–1.00 of them in every version so far.
Accepted / rejected
Not our opinion. A rule fixed before training decides; v03 is the first version it turned down.

What fell, and what did not. Sensitivity dropped by seven points, from 0.402 to 0.331. Event F1 went up, because the false-alarm rate fell further than sensitivity did. The four pre-registered hypotheses

Decided exactly as written in the pre-registration: all seizures of the locked set, per-patient averages, and P ≥ 0.90 on the patient bootstrap for H1, H2 and H4.

H2 is the one that did not hold. The direction was right and the effect was the size we expected; the bootstrap put the probability of improvement at 0.881, and our rule said 0.90. We are not going to round it. Seizures without movement remain the hardest case and the one we are least able to claim.

A finding of our own that did not replicate. On the development set we measured that the brain signal changes before the heart, before movement and before muscle — the timing argument that motivated putting EEG at the centre of the design. On the locked patients it did not hold: the brain signal led heart rate by −1.0 s (interval −15 to +6 s, 21 seizures), which is to say heart rate moved first, at the median. Movement and muscle still followed the brain. H3a is recorded as not held. The case for ear-EEG on this page now rests on the information it carries, which H4 and H1 both support, and not on it arriving first.

What making the model real-time costs. The causal lineage (v11) never looks forward in time. On the development set that cost 0.011 event F1, an interval that includes zero. On the locked patients it cost 0.073 [−0.135, −0.017] — an interval that does not include zero. Both numbers are from the same comparison, on different patients, and we publish both rather than the kinder one. The causal model is the one that could be worn; the offline model is the one that should be compared with published offline work.

Where it still misses. On the locked patients, model C caught 77 % of convulsive seizures (per patient; 18 of 22 counted directly), 32 % of focal seizures with movement, 17 % of focal seizures without movement, and none of the 30 seizures whose motor status was unrecorded. No seizure shorter than 15 seconds was caught, in any model. A third of a patient's seizures, found without a hospital bed, is a result worth having; it is not a monitor, and the page on ethics and limitations says what follows from that.

Reference point: the dataset's own authors, on the same data The full landscape →

KU Leuven's algorithm (behind-the-ear EEG + ECG, trained on SeizeIT1, tested on SeizeIT2: 192 adults, 616 focal seizures, 11,443 h; Swinnen et al., Epilepsia Open 2026) reaches sensitivity 0.73 on its own but at 6.2 false alarms per hour (≈ 149 per day, precision 0.004), so the published system adds a human reviewer: sensitivity 0.31, precision 0.83, F1 0.45, about 8 minutes of review per 24 h of data; FBTCS 0.82, focal impaired-awareness 0.37, focal aware 0.14, frontal onset 0.06. Their algorithm does not use EMG or movement. ICTRA v02 Model C, algorithm only, on all 8,642 development hours: 0.50 at 10 false alarms per day, 0.27 at 1 per day, FBTC 1.00. (v01 reported 0.44 at ≈ 10 per day, but counted false alarms only on recordings that contain a seizure, which flattered the rate.) Caveats that matter: theirs is a true cross-dataset test and ours is development-set cross-validation; the seizure sets and hour bases differ. The comparison becomes fair after our locked and external tests in November.

What v03b actually showed

v03b did fix what it was built to fix: the threshold’s seed-to-seed spread fell from about 0.019 to 0.0015. But at every fixed false-alarm budget the two versions sit on the same curve — at 1, 2, 5 and 10 alarms per 24 hours the sensitivities differ by at most 0.013. So v03b is not a worse model. It is the same model with its alarm threshold put in a worse place, and the pre-set rule rejected it for that.

The diagnostic found why, and v02g fixed it: the threshold search was pinned to one grid candidate in every run. Spacing the candidates by alarm rate instead raised event F1 from 0.19 to 0.28 and cut false alarms from 3.0 to 1.3 per day, with no change to the model. The entry under dead ends has the full account.

What is still not fixed. τ still moves between repeats — its spread per fold roughly halved, but across 15 repeats it still ranges from 0.961 to 0.994, and the false-alarm rate those repeats produce ranges from 0.01 to 8.95 per day. The number we report is the mean over repeats; a deployed device would need one threshold, and choosing it reliably is unfinished work.

What v03 taught us

The stability goal was met — the threshold's seed-to-seed spread shrank from 0.005–0.031 to 0.0002–0.0023, and false alarms halved — but the line settled slightly higher on the same curve and 6 % of seizures fell below it. The rule says that is too many, so v03 was not adopted, even though its F1 is the best we have seen. The fix is not a better model but a better way of choosing where on the curve to sit; that is v03b. Short seizures (< 15 s) lost most: 0.20 → 0.11. Logged in docs/ITERATION_LOG.md, commit 7848a71.

Why v01's false-alarm rate was too kind

v01 scored only the recordings that contain a seizure — about a fifth of the data. Real wearing is mostly quiet hours, and that is where false alarms live. v02 scores every hour, so its false-alarm rate is the one a patient would experience. From v02 onward, every comparison is made at a fixed false-alarm budget (1, 2, 5 and 10 per day): hold the budget equal, then ask which version catches more seizures. Comparing single tuned thresholds across versions, as the first two rows above do, is not fair and we will stop doing it.

What we can say today

Among non-surgical wearables, ICTRA is the only system that models all four signals together and reports results for every focal seizure type. On the shared SeizeIT2 data, its algorithm alone has an order of magnitude fewer false alarms than the dataset authors' published algorithm, and catches more convulsive seizures. At a matched budget of 1–2 false alarms a day, ICTRA's overall sensitivity (0.24, interim) sits about 7 points below the authors' reviewer-assisted figure (0.31) and 13 below the best 19-channel scalp-EEG algorithm (SzCORE 2025 winner, 0.37 at 1.3 FA/day) — the first gap is within statistical noise, the second is mostly electrode count.

What we do not say yet

That it beats the seizure watch on convulsive seizures (parity on the development set; the watch is FDA-cleared and validated prospectively), or that it beats anyone on unseen patients. Those claims wait for the locked 29 patients and the external datasets in November — and the numbers will be published whichever way they fall.

Dead ends and negative results, kept on purpose Constance · honesty layer

A result that goes against us is still a result. Everything below was tried, measured and written down before it was set aside; nothing here was deleted from the record.

Prediction instead of detection — considered, declined

Tried: a literature check (17 Oct 2026) on forecasting seizures minutes ahead from wearable signals. Found: the strongest study (Nasseri & Stirling, Epilepsia 2025: 11 patients, ~337 days of smartwatch data each, implanted EEG as ground truth) reaches AUC 0.74–0.78 with 6–8 hours per day in a warning state, after 2–3 months of per-patient training; HRV-based prediction (72-study review, 2024) is patient-specific and retrospective, with changes mostly inside the last 5 minutes; the only out-of-sample ECG test we found (Siena, 6 patients) fell to 70 % sensitivity at 3.4 false alarms per hour. SeizeIT2 (2–5 day stays, de-identified clocks) cannot test such a claim. Decision: stay with detection; call the seconds-level heart-rate lead what it is — early detection. Why detection, not prediction →

Exp P · pre-ictal drift in the last two minutes — tested, nothing found

Question: do heart rate, HRV, muscle or movement drift in the −120…−30 s before onset compared with −300…−120 s? Design: 538 development seizures (93 patients) with ≥ 10 min of clean recording before onset, each paired with 10 pseudo-onsets drawn from seizure-free parts of the same patient's recordings (5,350 controls). A signal counts as drifting only if the share of seizures with a z > 2 change exceeds the control share with a patient-bootstrap interval that excludes zero. Registered as exploratory before the run. Result (17 Oct 2026): 0 of 20 cells (4 signals × 5 groups) pass; 0 of 20 again with a −60…−30 s window. Median drift z is −0.13 to +0.08 for seizures — the same as for controls. Falling values are equally absent. The seizure and control curves overlap until about −20 s; the rise after that is the seizure starting around the annotated onset, which is what Exp R measured. Decision: a warning minutes ahead is not plausible from these four signals; ICTRA stays a detection system. Not ruled out: slow changes over 10–60 min, scalp-EEG precursors, clinical R-R HRV, and sleep/wake effects (the controls could not be matched for vigilance). results/expP/FINDINGS.md

Hypothesis H3 as first written — not supported

"EEG changes before heart rate in most seizures." On the development set the behind-the-ear EEG was strictly first in only 41–56 % of seizures by type (FBTC 54 %, focal motor 45 %, non-motor 41 %, unknown 56 %). Rewritten before pre-registration into two predictions the data can actually test on the locked set: H3a, FBTC is EEG-first; H3b, in focal seizures ECG adds coverage, not speed. The original wording stays in the iteration log. Outcome on the locked set: H3b held; H3a did not — the rewritten, narrower version of the claim failed too, on patients chosen in advance to judge it.

A ≥ 70 % threshold for H3b — rejected as circular

A first draft of H3b set a 70 % threshold for "heart rate within ±10 s of the EEG change". The development set gives 57 %, so the threshold would have been chosen by looking at the answer. Replaced with a model-level prediction (B's delay not shorter than A's by more than 2 s, B's sensitivity higher).

Seed-dependent decision threshold — found in v01, explained in v03b, fixed in v02g

The symptom (v01): one Model A fold produced 50.9 false alarms per 24 h on one seed while its two siblings stayed under 9. Model C did the same, with τ at 0.915 on one seed and 0.976 on another.

Two attempts that did not fix it: v03 chose τ by inner cross-validation, and v03b added a sensitivity floor. Both stabilised τ and both were rejected by the accept rule, because a stable threshold in the wrong place is still the wrong place.

The cause (found in the v03b diagnostic): the search tried 40 candidate thresholds spaced evenly between the 90th and 99.99th percentile of the model’s scores. Even spacing by percentile is very uneven in alarm rate — the last two candidates fire on 0.27 % and 0.01 % of windows, a 27-fold gap with nothing between them. All thirty learned-model runs picked the same candidate. The operating point was not being chosen from the data; it was pinned by the grid.

The fix (v02g): space the candidates by alarm rate instead. No change to the features, the model or its settings. Event F1 went from 0.19 to 0.28 and false alarms fell from 3.0 to 1.3 per day. The same grid had been in use since v01, so three earlier versions were reading their results from a point the search could not leave.

v03 · stable threshold — worked, rejected anyway

Tried: choosing the alarm threshold by inner cross-validation so it stops depending on the random seed. Found (18 Oct 2026): it worked — threshold spread fell 10×, false alarms halved (9.7 → 4.5/day), F1 rose 0.32 → 0.38. But sensitivity fell 0.44 → 0.38, three times more than the 0.02 the accept rule allows. Decision: rejected under §1 as written; the better F1 did not override the rule. Carried into v03b with a sensitivity floor. Exhibit 3 →

Datasets we did not use, and why

Three Kaggle "EMG seizure" datasets: one is a 126 GB verbatim re-upload of SeizeIT2 without attribution (and with someone's API key inside), one is an undocumented dump of five SeizeIT2 subjects, one generates random numbers in a notebook and scores 50 %. SeizeIT1 is no longer shared (ethics approval expired). EEGMMIDB, HBN and Bonn as "healthy negatives" were dropped because a model would learn which dataset a window came from, not whether it holds a seizure. A proposed sub-001…030 / 031…042 split was rejected because two patients held 130 of the 187 "test" seizures.

Numbers we will not report

Accuracy, specificity and false alarms per hour. On data where seizures are under 1 % of the time, accuracy is near 99 % for a model that never fires; FA per hour hides a device that cries wolf 24 times a day. Every table uses event sensitivity, FA per 24 h, delay and F1, by seizure type, with patient-bootstrap intervals.

ECG alone is not enough

On SeizeIT2, the best ECG-only detector reached 82.8% sensitivity but 26 false alarms per hour. The authors call false-alarm rates "substantially above clinically acceptable thresholds". This is the gap fusion closes.

Wearable seizure detection review2025
0.4FA / 24 h with EEG + EMG

Multimodal wearables cut false alarms

Two-channel behind-the-ear EEG alone: 100% sensitivity, 10.3 false alarms per day. Add EMG: 97.7% sensitivity, 0.4 per day. Add movement: 0.1 per day. Evidence that combining signals works.

Ictal tachycardia, a sharp heart-rate rise, occurs in roughly 80% of focal seizures and in a subset starts seconds before the EEG onset, especially in temporal lobe epilepsy.
